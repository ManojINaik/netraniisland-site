Is Scuba Diving Safe at Netrani? An Honest Guide for First-Timers

By Netrani Desk · Updated 16 November 2026 · 6 min read

Quick answers

  • PADI rates recreational diving as low-risk next to many sports, and says most injuries come from diver error.
  • PADI's try-dive standard is a 12 m maximum and up to four divers per instructor; ask what your dive will run at.
  • The main beginner risks are ear pain, panic, seasickness, sun and dehydration; never hold your breath.
  • Boats stay in when the sea is unsafe, and we move your trip to the next safe day at no extra cost.
  • No alcohol on the dive day, and wait at least 12 hours before flying after a single dive.

For most healthy beginners, scuba diving at Netrani is safe when the dive is run properly and you follow the briefing. PADI describes recreational diving as low-risk compared with many other outdoor sports, and the most common problems it lists are sunburn, seasickness and dehydration. Serious injuries are uncommon, and PADI says most of them come from diver error, which is exactly what the briefing and the rules are there to prevent.

How safe your own dive is comes down to three things: your health on the day, the state of the sea, and how carefully the dive is run.

What keeps a try dive safe?

PADI sets clear standards for its Discover Scuba Diving try dives:

  • Depth: a maximum of 12 m in open water, and never deeper than you are comfortable with.
  • Group size: no more than four divers per instructor in open water, with extra limits when 10 or 11 year olds are in the group, and fewer whenever conditions call for it.
  • Supervision: the instructor must stay close enough to reach you, adjust your buoyancy and help at any moment, and should not be busy taking photos or videos while supervising you.
  • Screening: a medical questionnaire before the dive, and a doctor's sign-off when your answers call for one.

Those are the standards on paper. On a busy weekend boat, ask how many divers your instructor will take down and who will be taking the photos.

What does the briefing teach you?

Rule Why it matters
Breathe slowly and never hold your breath Air expands as you rise, and holding it on the way up can injure the lungs
Clear your ears early and often Divers Alert Network notes most authorities advise equalising about every 0.6 m on the way down, before it hurts
If your ears hurt, stop and rise a little PADI's rule is never to force it; try again a metre higher
Learn the OK signal: thumb and finger in a circle A thumbs-up underwater means go up, not all good
Answer every OK signal Your instructor checks on you throughout the dive
Come up slowly PADI recommends a three-minute safety stop at about 5 m as an extra precaution

Before you go down, you practise breathing through the mouthpiece and using the jacket to float or sink, at the surface or in shallow water. Take as long as you need there. The first-dive checklist covers the rest of the morning.

What are the real risks for a beginner?

Risk Usual cause How you avoid it
Ear pain Going down without equalising Equalise early, stop and rise a little if it hurts, never force it
Lung injury Holding your breath while coming up Breathe slowly and continuously, all the time
Panic Water in the mask, fear, a rushed start Signal, hold on to your instructor, slow your breathing, come up together
Seasickness The open-sea crossing A light breakfast, a seat facing the horizon, telling the crew early
Sunburn and dehydration Hours on an open deck Sunscreen, a cap and plenty of water
Cuts and stings Touching rock, coral or animals Look, do not touch
Heart trouble Existing conditions An honest medical form and a doctor's check if needed

That last row is why the medical form asks so many questions about the heart. PADI, quoting Divers Alert Network's annual report, notes that about half of diving deaths are associated with an acute heart event, often in older divers with existing problems such as high blood pressure, diabetes or smoking. If you are over 45, read the Netrani scuba age limit guide before booking. If you have a cold, blocked ears or a recent illness, see who should skip a dive.

Tiredness feeds seasickness and nerves, so try not to step off an overnight bus straight onto the boat. Sleeping in Murudeshwar the night before helps, and our sister site has a stay guide for early scuba reporting.

Why do some Netrani trips get cancelled?

The sea decides. The crossing is about 19 km of open water, and the crew checks conditions every morning. High waves, strong wind or a storm warning keep the boats in, sometimes at short notice. When that happens, we move you to the next safe day at no extra cost.

Every monsoon the Uttara Kannada district administration bans sea water sports outright, with dates that change from year to year. Rough days can also come in the middle of the season, so if the dive is the main reason for your trip, keep a spare morning in your plan. How do you choose a careful dive operator?

You do not need to know dive centres by name to judge them. Ask these questions before you pay; a careful team answers them without fuss.

  • Permission: is the agency one of those the district administration permits to dive at Netrani?
  • Instructor: what is your instructor's PADI or SSI number? PADI can confirm whether a PADI instructor is current and allowed to teach, and its own advice is that hesitation over the number is a red flag.
  • Group size: how many divers will each instructor take down on your dive?
  • Medical form: will you fill in the questionnaire before diving, and will they say no if your answers need a doctor?
  • Briefing: is there a proper briefing and surface practice before you go down?
  • Emergency kit: is there emergency oxygen and a first-aid kit on the boat? Divers Alert Network recommends that every dive operator carry an emergency oxygen kit on board.
  • Life jackets: is there one for everyone on board, including non-divers?
  • Weather calls: will they cancel when the sea is rough rather than push on?
  • Photos: who takes the underwater photos? Ideally, someone other than the instructor watching you.

Walk away if there is no medical form, if the briefing is rushed or skipped, if there is alcohol on board, or if you are pressed to dive after saying you feel unwell. When you book through us, you can put any of these questions to the desk before you pay.

What should you do after the dive?

  • Flying: Divers Alert Network advises waiting at least 12 hours after a single dive before you fly, and at least 18 hours after several dives or several days of diving.
  • Alcohol: none until all your diving is done. Drink water instead; DAN notes that dehydration is a possible risk factor for decompression sickness.
  • Ears: pain, ringing or hearing loss after a dive means no more diving until a doctor has checked you.
  • Anything else: if you feel unwell after the dive, tell the crew straight away and see a doctor.

Questions travellers ask

What is the biggest risk in scuba diving?

For a first-timer, the risk to guard against is panic: bolting for the surface or holding your breath on the way up, which can injure the lungs. That is why the briefing drills slow, steady breathing and clear signals. Your health matters too, which is why the medical form asks so much about the heart and lungs.

Do divers breathe 100% oxygen?

No. Recreational scuba tanks hold filtered, compressed air, about 21 per cent oxygen, the same mix you are breathing now. PADI explains that pure oxygen turns toxic under pressure, so recreational divers never breathe it underwater; on the boat it is kept for first aid.

What is the 120 rule in diving?

An old rule of thumb from the days of US Navy dive tables: depth in feet plus time at that depth in minutes should not add up to more than 120. It was only ever a rough guide for a certified diver's first dive of the day, and divers now plan with a dive computer or tables. On a try dive your instructor manages depth and time, so you never need to work it out.

Are there sharks at Netrani?

Divers and journalists have reported blacktip and whitetip reef sharks around the island, but sightings are occasional, and most divers would count one as a lucky moment. PADI points out that dogs, snakes and crocodiles kill more people every year than sharks do.

What happens if my ears hurt during the dive?

Stop going down and signal your instructor. Rising a metre or so and trying again often helps; PADI's rule is never to force it. If your ears still will not clear, the dive ends there, and that is the right call, not a failure.

Is scuba diving safe for non-swimmers?

It can be, on a guided try dive, if you are relaxed in water and can keep yourself afloat at the surface. There is no swim test for a try dive, and the instructor is with you the whole time. If open water frightens you, start with snorkelling.
